# The Hero Theory: Why Users Trust, Stay, or Leave

*Status: draft hypothesis, not yet tested against real users.*

The Hero Theory says a product wins by doing one job for one group better than the product they already use. It is built by looking at the ordinary user, not the founder. It came out of a conversation about why people trust a product, keep using it, or leave it.

## Rules 1 and 2: start with the hero, and find the core ingredient

Do not start with "I want to build a chat app." Start by asking, in this order:

1. Who are the heroes, meaning what do people already use for this?
2. Where do those heroes hurt users? Count only pain users actually feel, not pain you can list.
3. Who are the users who feel that pain?
4. Only then, what should I build?

Every product also has a core ingredient, like the one thing that makes rice cooked rice. It is the one job or feeling the product must deliver. If it is missing, nothing else matters. Everything around it can change, the way a benzene ring stays benzene when one hydrogen is swapped.

## Rule 3: users come in four types

Each type opens the app for a different reason, so each is won and lost differently. Real people are often a mix, but one type usually dominates.

| Type | Why they open the app | Won by | Lost by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Task | To get something done | A result that works every time | One failure at a bad moment |
| Trigger | A feeling such as tired or bored | A fast relief with no effort | Effort or boredom |
| Crowd | Their group is there | The group moving together | The group leaving |
| Anchor | One cherished person is there | That person staying | That person leaving |

A quick test separates trigger from crowd: ask "if all your friends deleted it tomorrow, would you still open it?" A trigger user says yes, a crowd user says no.

## Rules 4 to 6: trust, habit, exit, and where first users come from

Three forces decide whether a user stays.

- **Trust:** proof from the user's own experience, not promises.
- **Habit:** a trigger or a task that keeps repeating.
- **Exit:** there are three causes. Forced exit comes from life outside the product (a parent, a ban, no data). Fade means the need ended. Betrayal means the product hurt the user, through ads, scams or lost data. Only betrayal is the product's fault.

First users come from three groups, and none of them requires beating the hero everywhere:

- **People with no hero yet:** new students, new city, new job. No habit to break.
- **People who want a second hero:** they keep the old app and add yours for one job it does badly.
- **People hurt with nowhere to go:** they join if moving is easy (bring my data, bring my people).

Matching the hero's stability and speed is only the entry ticket, because equal is not a reason to switch. You still need one thing clearly better for one group. Size does not decide it, fit does, the way Earth fits life better than the much larger Jupiter.

## Related ideas

This is not claimed as new. It overlaps with "jobs to be done" (the core ingredient), network effects (crowd and anchor users) and switching costs. The parts I think are my own angle are the ordinary-user starting point, the four user types, and the anchor idea.

## Case study: applying it to Lumora

Most of Lumora's users are probably anchor users, and the founder is the anchor. The admin panel on Oct 12, 2026 shows 93 total users and 5 active in the last 7 days, about 5%. People signed up through his posts and shares, tried it, and mostly did not return, so the attachment has not moved from the person to the product.

The weak point is that "all in one" is a feature list, not a job. A new user does not wake up wanting an ecosystem. They want one thing done. This is a reading to test, not a proven fact.

### Hypothesis to test: Ajo as the first hero to challenge

Ajo is the sub-app that fits the theory best, because it is a task with a real need that brings users in as groups. Pick one hero, one pain and one group, and see whether it holds before spreading out. Nothing here is proven yet.

- **Task:** people already run savings circles and group contributions, so the job exists.
- **Crowd:** one circle owner can bring 8 to 20 people at once, instead of users arriving one by one.
- **Hero:** probably WhatsApp groups plus notebooks or screenshots, where tracking gets messy. This still needs to be confirmed by asking people.
- **Habit:** payment reminders create built-in repeat use.
- **Low data:** Lumora's small download size helps the group hurt by heavy apps.

## The 14-day test plan

The theory only improves when real users confirm or break it, so the next step is a short test, not more theory.

1. **Tag your links.** Use `?ref=status`, `?ref=reddit` and `?ref=school`, so each signup shows where it came from and the question "does posting a status raise signups?" gets a real answer.
2. **Find the 5 active users** in the Users page of the admin panel. Ask each: "What made you open Lumora last time? If I stopped posting, would you still use it?" Sort the answers into task, trigger, crowd or anchor.
3. **Ask 5 people who run savings groups or contributions** how they track it now and what annoys them. This tests the Ajo hypothesis.
4. **Track anchor transfer.** After 4 weeks, check whether users still show up on days the founder did not post.
